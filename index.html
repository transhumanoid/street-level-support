A public ethos

Street-level support

Meet people where they are. Help with what they say they need. If the official story and the person in front of you disagree, check the person first.

This page is a plain-language guide to a way of working with people in distress that starts on the street, not in a waiting room. It is Michael's distilled public ethos — not a clinical manual, not legal advice, and not a substitute for care.

A home is not a prize for getting well. A key is not a gold star. It is the ground that everything else stands on.

Principles

  1. Start with the street, not the system. Ask whether this person is safe tonight, has a key, has eaten, is being hunted, or wants company. Paperwork comes later.
  2. Use the inverse rule. When a mainstream story sounds tidy — "they refuse help," "they need to be ready," "treatment first, housing later" — turn it around and test the opposite before you accept the official version.
  3. Do not make housing a reward for recovery. Housing First gives people a stable place to live without making sobriety or treatment a condition of the keys, then offers support. Large public trials, including Canada's At Home/Chez Soi project, found that people housed this way spend much more of their time in stable housing than people left in usual services.
  4. Make change possible, not lectured. Contingency management is a plain idea with a strong research base, especially around cocaine and methamphetamine use: a reachable goal, a fair check, and a real reward that arrives soon after.
  5. Count the whole bill. Cutting cheap, early, human support often shows up later as hospital beds, police calls, evictions, and jail. That is the false economy of many mental health systems.
  6. Treat lived experience as evidence, not decoration. Hire people who have been through the system, listen to them, and let them shape the work.
  7. Remember that rooms are not the only place people get well. Time on the water, time outdoors, and skilled hand work can settle a nervous system when forms cannot. Call it ecotherapy or blue water therapy if a name helps.
  8. If you build tools, including AI, design them as support, not as a judge. Help a person think, remember, plan, or reach a human being. Do not diagnose in secret, shame, or replace the relationship.
  9. Keep a sense of humour, and keep the story honest. Life is often closer to a comedy skit than a case file. Laugh with people, never at them, and do not tidy the plot.

How to apply this

  • Ask "what would make tonight safer?" before you ask "what is your diagnosis?"
  • Offer a key, a meal, a lift, a quiet hour, or a real choice before you offer a program.
  • If someone says no, treat that as information about the offer, not as proof they do not want help.
  • When a meeting is about a person who is not in the room, stop and change that if you can.
  • Follow public evidence where it is strong, instead of habit.
  • Build support around ordinary life: a boat, a bench, a studio, a kitchen, a walk.
  • If you write software or prompts for mental health support, make the limits obvious, keep humans in the loop, and never harvest private distress without clear, informed consent.

What to avoid

  • Making housing, benefits, or basic care depend on compliance, insight, or sobriety first.
  • Treating "they declined the service" as the end of the story.
  • Inventing numbers, or quoting "evidence" you have not actually read.
  • Using a person's crisis, family, or legal situation as content.
  • Building AI that pretends to be a therapist, a friend, or an authority it is not.
  • Speaking for people when they can speak for themselves.
  • Saving money in a clinic budget by pushing costs onto streets, hospitals, and cells.

Public evidence

Do not take these ideas on trust. The housing and incentive claims above come from public research you can read yourself:

  • At Home/Chez Soi, Mental Health Commission of Canada
  • National At Home/Chez Soi final report
  • Research review of Housing First and the Canadian trial (people in Housing First spent about 73% of their time in stable housing, compared with about 32% in usual services)
  • Pathways Housing First Institute
  • NIDA: treatment and recovery, including contingency management
  • NIDA: cocaine, including contingency management

Other public threads

This ethos also lives in public, non-clinical work: converting a 1945 timber Navy vessel into a liveaboard yacht; dichroic glasswork, which shifts colour with the light; and an autobiography concept, "My Life as a Comedy Skit." Those projects are part of the same idea: a life is larger than a service plan.
