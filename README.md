# Street-Level Support

A short public guide to a way of working with people in distress that starts on the street, not in a waiting room.

This is Michael's distilled ethos, written for anyone who wants to use it. It is not a clinical manual, not legal advice, and not a substitute for care. Take what is useful. Leave the rest.

Site: [transhumanoid.github.io/street-level-support](https://transhumanoid.github.io/street-level-support/). Source: [github.com/transhumanoid/street-level-support](https://github.com/transhumanoid/street-level-support).

## The core idea

Meet people where they are. Help with what they say they need. Do not make housing, food, or basic safety depend on them first agreeing with a diagnosis, a program, or a story about themselves.

If the official story and the person in front of you disagree, check the person first.

## Principles

1. **Start with the street, not the system.** The most useful information is often what you can see and hear in ordinary life: is this person safe tonight, do they have a key, have they eaten, is anyone hunting them, do they want company. Paperwork comes later.

2. **The inverse rule.** When a mainstream story sounds tidy — "they refuse help," "they need to be ready," "treatment first, housing later" — turn it around and test the opposite. Maybe the help on offer is the wrong shape. Maybe "not ready" means the door has a lock they cannot open. Check before you accept the official version.

3. **A home is not a prize for getting well.** Housing First is a well-studied approach: give people a stable place to live, without making sobriety or treatment a condition of the keys, and then offer support. Canada's At Home/Chez Soi project, the world's largest trial of this model, followed more than 2,000 people in five cities for two years. A published review of that trial found that people offered Housing First spent about 73% of their time in stable housing, compared with about 32% for people left in usual services. Read the [Mental Health Commission of Canada's account](https://mentalhealthcommission.ca/what-we-do/at-home/) and the [research review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4679127/). The original New York model is described by the [Pathways Housing First Institute](https://www.pathwayshousingfirst.org/).

4. **People change when change is possible, not when they are lectured.** Contingency management is a plain idea with a strong research base, especially around cocaine and methamphetamine use: offer a clear, immediate, honest reward when someone meets a goal they can actually reach. The U.S. National Institute on Drug Abuse describes this approach in its [guide to treatment and recovery](https://nida.nih.gov/publications/drugs-brains-behavior-science-addiction/treatment-recovery) and on its [cocaine page](https://nida.nih.gov/research-topics/cocaine).

5. **Count the whole bill, not the program's budget.** Cutting cheap, early, human support often shows up later as hospital beds, police calls, evictions, and jail. That is the false economy of many mental health systems: they save money in the wrong column and spend more in the ones they do not own.

6. **Lived experience is evidence, not decoration.** People who have been through the system can see the traps that look like policy from the inside of an office. Hire them, listen to them, and let them shape the work. Do not extract their story for a brochure and then ignore their advice.

7. **Water, light, and making things are not extras.** Time on the water, time outdoors, and skilled hand work can settle a nervous system when rooms and forms cannot. Call it ecotherapy or blue water therapy if you need a name. The point is simple: some people get well in a workshop or on a deck, not in a clinic chair.

8. **If you build tools, including AI, design them as support, not as a judge.** A useful tool helps a person think, remember, plan, or reach a human being. It should not diagnose in secret, shame, or replace the relationship. Keep the person in charge of their own story.

9. **Keep a sense of humour, and keep the story honest.** Life is often closer to a comedy skit than a case file. "My Life as a Comedy Skit" is one way of telling a life without turning it into a tragedy others can manage. Laugh with people, never at them, and do not tidy the plot.

## How to apply this

- Ask "what would make tonight safer?" before you ask "what is your diagnosis?"
- Offer a key, a meal, a lift, a quiet hour, or a real choice before you offer a program.
- If someone says no, treat that as information about the offer, not as proof they do not want help.
- When a meeting is about a person who is not in the room, stop and change that if you can.
- Follow public evidence where it is strong (housing without preconditions; clear, fair rewards for reachable goals) instead of habit.
- Build support around ordinary life: a boat, a bench, a studio, a kitchen, a walk.
- If you write software or prompts for mental health support, make the limits obvious, keep humans in the loop, and never harvest private distress for training without clear, informed consent.

## What to avoid

- Making housing, benefits, or basic care depend on compliance, insight, or sobriety first.
- Treating "they declined the service" as the end of the story.
- Inventing numbers, or quoting "evidence" you have not actually read.
- Using a person's crisis, family, or legal situation as content.
- Building AI that pretends to be a therapist, a friend, or an authority it is not.
- Speaking for people when they can speak for themselves.
- Saving money in a clinic budget by pushing costs onto streets, hospitals, and cells.
