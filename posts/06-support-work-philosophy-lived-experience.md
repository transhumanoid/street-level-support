# Been There Counts

Why lived experience belongs at the centre of support work

Some of the most useful help a person can get comes from someone who has been through something similar. Not a perfect copy of their story, but close enough to say "I know that road, and I'm still here."

This essay follows one thread of the street-level approach: putting people with lived experience of mental distress, homelessness, or addiction at the centre of support, not at the edge of it.

## What lived experience brings

A person who has been through the system knows things a manual cannot teach.

They know what a waiting room feels like when you have not slept. They know which forms make people give up. They know how it feels to be talked about as a "case" while you are sitting right there. They often notice the small thing that would actually help: a phone charger, a lift to an appointment, someone who waits with you.

That knowledge is not a soft extra. It is expertise. It just came from a different school.

## Hope you can see

When things are bad, "it gets better" can sound like a line from a poster. It sounds different from someone who was where you are and is now doing okay. They are living proof, not a promise.

This is the idea behind peer support work, which many services now pay for. Peer workers use their own recovery, in a careful and purposeful way, to support others. A good public introduction is the [SAMHSA page on peer support](https://www.samhsa.gov/brss-tacs/recovery-support-tools/peers), from the US government's mental health agency.

## Nothing about us without us

Advocacy groups have used the phrase "nothing about us without us" for a long time. It means decisions about a group of people should include those people.

In practice, that means:

- People with lived experience help design services, not just fill in a feedback form after.
- They sit on the boards and panels that set priorities and spend money.
- They are paid for their time and knowledge, like any other expert.
- Their input changes things. If it never changes anything, it is decoration.

## Apply the inverse rule

The usual story is that professionals hold the knowledge and service users receive it. The inverse rule says: check the opposite.

What if the people using the service know things the people running it do not? Often they do. They see the gaps, the delays, and the moments where people drop out, because they lived those moments. A service that never asks them is flying with one eye shut.

## Care for the carers

Lived-experience work asks a lot. Peer workers can be pulled back into hard memories. They can be treated as cheap labour or as a token. If services want this work, they need to back it properly: fair pay, real supervision, clear roles, and time off when it gets heavy.

Using someone's hardest years as a resource and then giving them no support is not inclusion. It is extraction.

## Try this

- If you run a service, ask people who use it what one thing would make the biggest difference. Then do it, and tell them you did.
- Pay people with lived experience for their time and advice.
- If you are a worker, notice when you are talking about someone instead of with them.
- If you have been through it yourself, your experience is worth something. You do not have to share it, but if you choose to, it can help.
- Measure whether people's input changed anything. If it did not, fix that first.
