# Not Sick Enough, Until Too Sick

How mental health systems wait for a crisis, and what it would take to stop

Many people who ask for mental health help hear some version of the same answer: "You are not unwell enough for our service." A few months later, the same person may be told the opposite: "You are too unwell for us. You need the hospital."

Between those two answers is a gap. People fall into it every day. This essay is about that gap, why systems keep building it, and what a street-level approach would do instead.

## The door that only opens in a crisis

Most services have an entry test. It makes sense on paper: there are limited places, so the people most at risk go first.

But look at what that rule teaches. If help only arrives when things are very bad, then the quickest route to help is to get worse, or to say things are worse than they are. People learn to wait. Families learn to wait. Workers learn to say "come back if it gets worse."

A system that only opens in a crisis will keep producing crises. Not on purpose, but reliably.

## The inverse rule: who is the system really serving?

Michael's habit is to check the opposite of the official story. The official story is: "Entry rules protect scarce help for the people who need it most."

Turn it over. Ask who the rule protects. Often it protects the service: its waiting list looks shorter, its staff are less stretched, and its numbers look tidy. The person who was turned away does not appear in those numbers at all. They show up later, somewhere else, usually in worse shape.

That does not make the people running services bad. Most are doing their best inside rules they did not write. It means the rules deserve the same hard look we give to the people trying to get through the door.

## Measuring the wrong thing

Systems tend to count what is easy to count: how many people were seen, how long the wait was, how fast people were discharged. Those numbers can all improve while people get worse.

A short wait means little if the answer at the end is "no." A fast discharge looks efficient until the same person comes back through emergency a few weeks later. When a number becomes the goal, people find ways to hit the number. There is an old idea for this, often called Goodhart's law: once a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure.

Better questions are harder to put in a spreadsheet, but not impossible:

- Did the person have somewhere safe to sleep a month later?
- Did they have at least one person they could call?
- Did they come back to the service because it helped, or because it was the only door left?

## What the gap costs

The earlier essay in this series, *The Cheapest Help Is the Help You Cut*, looked at money: savings in one budget turning into bigger costs in another. The "not sick enough" gap is the human side of the same pattern.

While someone waits, they may lose a job, a lease, a relationship, or their trust that asking for help is worth it. Those losses are hard to win back. Some of them are the very things that would have kept the person well.

## What a street-level approach does instead

None of this needs a new building. It mostly needs a different starting point.

**Help comes before the label.** You do not need a diagnosis to need a meal, a bed, a phone charge, or someone to talk to. Start there.

**Every "no" comes with a next step.** If a service cannot help, the person leaves with a real name, a real number, and ideally a warm handover, not a pamphlet.

**Someone stays.** One worker who keeps showing up, even when the person does not fit any program, is worth more than three referrals that go nowhere.

**Peers at the front door.** People with lived experience are often best placed to spot the person who is quietly getting worse while "not meeting criteria."

**Count what matters.** Track whether people are housed, connected, and coming back by choice. Report the people who were turned away, not just the ones who were seen.

## Something you can do this week

If you work in a service, take one recent "not eligible" decision and follow it up. Where did that person go next? Did anyone find out? The answer will tell you more about your system than any monthly report.

If you are on the outside, keep asking for help, and ask for the next step in writing. You are not a burden for needing help before things fall apart. That is exactly when help works best.
