# Waiting Is Not Free

Why every week someone spends waiting for a home is a week somebody is paying for

Earlier essays in this series made the case for Housing First (*Keys Before Conditions*) and for keeping people in the homes they already have (*Keeping a Home Is Cheaper Than Finding One*). This one looks at the bit in the middle: the wait.

## The quiet assumption

When there are not enough homes, people go on a list. The list feels neutral. Nobody is being harmed by a list, the thinking goes. They are just waiting their turn, and waiting costs nothing.

Michael's habit is to check the opposite of the usual story. So turn it over: what if waiting is one of the most expensive things a system can do?

## What a wait actually looks like

For a person without a home, waiting is not sitting still. It often means:

- moving between couches, cars, motels, shelters, and the street
- losing documents, phones, medication, and belongings with each move
- missing appointments because the address keeps changing
- health problems getting worse because they are hard to manage without a fridge, a bed, or a lock on the door
- support workers spending their time finding the person again instead of helping them

None of this stops while the name is on the list. It just builds up.

## Where the bill lands

This essay will not put numbers on it, because the real costs differ from place to place, and made-up figures help nobody. But the pattern is easy to see.

Emergency and temporary accommodation is often paid night by night, and in many places it costs more per night than ordinary rent would. Health care tends to arrive late, in emergency departments, instead of early, at a local clinic. Police and courts get pulled in for things that are really about having nowhere to be. Every one of these costs sits in a different budget, so no single person ever sees the full bill for one long wait.

That is the false economy again. Not spending money on homes does not save the money. It moves it somewhere harder to see, and usually somewhere more expensive.

Housing First programs have been studied in a number of countries, including large trials in Canada, and a common finding is that people placed quickly into housing with support tend to stay housed. A good plain-language starting point is the Canadian [At Home/Chez Soi project](https://www.mentalhealthcommission.ca/English/at-home) run by the Mental Health Commission of Canada.

## The human interest on a debt

A useful way to think about it: a wait is like a debt that charges interest. The longer it runs, the more it costs, and the cost is not only money. Trust wears down. Health slides. Small problems become big ones. By the time a home is finally offered, the person may need much more support than they would have on day one.

## What a street-level approach does

**Shorten the gap wherever you can.** Even when long-term homes are scarce, faster paperwork, ready-to-go furniture packs, and quick sign-offs can cut days or weeks.

**Keep the person findable.** A stable contact point, a phone that stays charged, a worker who knows their usual spots. Losing touch is one of the biggest hidden costs of waiting.

**Protect what they have.** Safe storage for documents and belongings stops people starting from zero every time they move.

**Count the cost of the wait, not just the cost of the home.** When a service argues for more housing, the real comparison is not "a home versus nothing". It is "a home versus everything the wait is already costing".

**Treat the wait as support time.** Use it to sort out ID, income, health appointments, and a plan for move-in day, so the first weeks in a new place go well.

## Something you can do this week

If you work in housing or support, pick one person who is waiting and ask: "What has this wait cost you so far?" Write the answer down. Those stories are evidence too.

If you are the one waiting, keep copies of your important documents somewhere safe, such as with a worker or in an email to yourself, and tell your worker the best way to reach you. It will not make the list shorter, but it can make the wait less costly.
