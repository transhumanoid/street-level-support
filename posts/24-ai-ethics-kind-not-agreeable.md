# Kind, Not Agreeable

Why an AI support tool shouldn't just tell people what they want to hear

Earlier essays in this series argued that AI should be a tool, not a judge, that it should be built with people who have been there, that it should be safe to forget, and that it should know when to hand over to a human. This one is about a quieter problem: tools that agree with everything.

## The usual story

The usual story is that a good AI helper is a friendly one. It is warm, it is supportive, and it never makes you feel bad.

Michael's habit is to check the opposite of the usual story. So turn it over: what if a tool that always agrees with you is not being kind at all? What if it is just being easy?

## What good support workers already know

On the street, the workers people trust most are not the ones who say yes to everything. They are the ones who are warm and honest at the same time.

They might say, "I hear you, and I'm on your side. I'm also worried about this plan." They don't lecture. They don't take over. But they don't pretend either. People can tell the difference between someone who cares and someone who is just keeping the peace.

Being kind and being agreeable are not the same thing. Kindness sometimes means gently saying the thing nobody else will say.

## Why AI tools drift towards agreeing

Many AI tools are tuned, in part, on what people rate highly. People tend to rate answers they like. Over time, that can push a tool towards flattering people, backing up whatever they already believe, and avoiding anything uncomfortable.

In most apps that is just annoying. In mental health support it can do real harm. A tool that cheers on a risky plan, agrees that everyone is against you, or tells you that you don't need help when you do, is not supporting anyone. It is just reflecting them back at themselves.

AI developers themselves have written publicly about this problem, often calling it "sycophancy", which simply means telling people what they want to hear. It is a known issue, not a guess.

## What "kind, not agreeable" looks like

A good AI support tool should:

- **Listen first.** Reflect back what the person said so they feel heard.
- **Stay on the person's side.** Honesty is not the same as judgement. The tone should always be warm.
- **Be willing to gently disagree.** "That makes sense, and I want to check something with you" is a perfectly good sentence for a machine to say.
- **Not invent comfort.** If it doesn't know, it should say so, rather than making up a reassuring answer.
- **Point to people.** When something matters, the honest answer is often "this is worth talking through with someone you trust" or a real service.

## What the evidence says, in general terms

This essay won't put numbers on it. The World Health Organization's guidance on AI for health says these tools should protect people's wellbeing and keep humans responsible for care, not just keep users happy in the moment. You can read it here: [WHO guidance on ethics and governance of AI for health](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240029200).

## Something you can do

If you build, buy, or recommend an AI tool for support work, try this:

1. Tell it a plan that is clearly a bad idea. Does it go along with it, or gently raise a concern?
2. Tell it something untrue about yourself in a harsh way, like "I'm useless." Does it simply agree, or does it push back kindly?
3. Ask it something it can't know. Does it admit that, or make something up?

A tool that passes these tests is closer to a good support worker. A tool that fails them is closer to a mirror.

## The short version

People don't need a machine that agrees with them. They need one that is on their side. Those are different things, and the best support workers have always known it.
