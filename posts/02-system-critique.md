# The Cheapest Help Is the Help You Cut

A plain look at the false economy of mental health systems

Most mental health systems are not short of effort. They are short of the right effort at the right time. The help that is cheapest to give, early and close to ordinary life, is often the first thing cut. The help that is most expensive, late and in a crisis, is the part that keeps running, because nobody is allowed to say no to an emergency.

This is a note on that pattern, written for support workers, managers, and anyone who has watched a person fall through a gap that was "saving money."

## Saving in one column, spending in another

A service is usually judged by its own budget. If a community program closes, the program's line in the spreadsheet goes to zero, and someone gets to report a saving.

But the person who used that program has not gone anywhere. Without a drop-in, a worker who knows them, or a place to sleep, the same person may end up in an emergency department, a police car, a court, a hospital ward, or a cell. Those costs land in someone else's budget. Nobody adds the columns together, so the saving looks real.

That is the false economy: money saved where it is visible, and spent several times over where it is not.

## The inverse rule, applied to the budget

Michael's habit is to check the opposite of the tidy story. The tidy story here is: "We cannot afford more early support."

Turn it over: can we afford not to? Ask what happens to the people who would have used that support. Follow them for a year. Where do they turn up, and who pays? If the honest answer is "hospital, police, and the courts," then the cut was not a saving. It was a transfer, usually to the most expensive and least humane parts of the system.

## Where the evidence points

You do not need to take this on faith. Housing First is one of the best-studied examples. In Canada's At Home/Chez Soi trial, people offered a home without preconditions stayed housed far more of the time than people left in usual services, and the project looked closely at what that meant for other services such as hospitals and shelters. You can read the [Mental Health Commission of Canada's final report](https://mentalhealthcommission.ca/resource/national-at-home-chez-soi-final-report/) and a [published research review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4679127/).

The general lesson from that kind of work is simple: stable, early support often costs less than it first appears, because it prevents some of the expensive things that would otherwise happen. Exact numbers vary by place and by person, so check local data before you quote a figure. Do not make one up.

## How the false economy shows up on the ground

- **Waiting lists as a filter.** A long wait looks like "managing demand." In practice it often means people get help only once things are bad enough to skip the queue.
- **Eligibility rules that require the problem to get worse.** "Not unwell enough for us" and "too unwell for us" can both be true at once, depending on which door you knock on.
- **Short contracts.** Programs funded for a year or two lose the staff and trust that made them work, then have to rebuild from nothing.
- **Discharge to nowhere.** Leaving a hospital or a cell without a home or a contact person is one of the fastest ways back in.
- **Counting contacts, not outcomes.** If a service is paid for appointments rather than for people staying well and housed, it will produce appointments.

## What to do instead

1. **Count the whole bill.** When you argue for a service, follow the person, not the program. Ask other agencies what they spend when your service is missing.
2. **Fund the boring basics.** A key, a phone, a meal, transport, a worker who answers. These rarely make headlines, and they hold a lot up.
3. **Make help easy to get early.** Fewer forms at the front door. No requirement to prove you are in crisis before you are allowed support.
4. **Pay for continuity.** Longer funding, the same worker where possible, and a warm handover instead of a referral letter.
5. **Bring in people with lived experience.** They usually know exactly where the costs leak out, because they have been the cost.
6. **Never discharge to the street if you can help it.** Planning where someone sleeps is part of care, not an extra.

## A note on humour

There is something absurd about a system that will pay for an ambulance but not for a bus fare, or for a ward bed but not for a rental bond. Michael's autobiography concept, "My Life as a Comedy Skit," starts from that absurdity. Naming it with a straight face, or a laugh, is often the first step to fixing it.

## The short version

The cheapest help is usually early, ordinary, and human. When it is cut, the cost does not vanish. It moves, and it usually grows. Before you accept that a cut is a saving, follow the person and add up the whole bill.
