# The Pilot That Never Grows Up

Notes on why good programs so often stay "trials" forever, and why short-term funding quietly costs more than it saves

There is a pattern that anyone who has worked in community support will recognise. A new program starts. It is small, local, and run by people who care. It works. People who were stuck start to move. Then the funding period ends, the program closes, and a year or two later someone starts a "new" trial that looks a lot like the old one.

This essay is about that pattern and why it is a false economy.

## How it happens

Most of the money for community support comes in short blocks: one year, two years, sometimes three. Each block has to be applied for, reported on, and justified again. That is meant to keep spending careful. In practice it often does the opposite.

- **Staff leave before the end.** Good workers can't wait to find out if their job exists next year, so they move on. The program loses the very relationships that made it work.
- **Trust takes longer than a grant.** People who have been let down before don't open up in the first few months. By the time they do, the program is often winding up.
- **Time goes on paperwork.** Applying for the next round and reporting on this one takes hours that could go to the people the program is for.
- **Lessons get lost.** When a program closes, what it learned usually leaves with the staff.

## Checking the opposite

Our usual habit applies: when everyone says "short trials keep spending careful", check the opposite.

What if the short-term model is the expensive one? Every closure means the next program pays again to hire, train, find a building, and earn trust from scratch. People who were making progress drop back, and often end up in emergency departments, police cells, or on the street, which are some of the most costly places for a society to meet someone in trouble.

Public evaluations of approaches like Housing First tend to make the same point: much of the benefit shows up over years, not months, as people stay housed and use fewer crisis services. A program that is judged after twelve months may be judged before its main results have had time to show.

## What steadier support looks like

None of this means every program should be funded forever no matter what. It means matching the funding to the kind of work:

- **Fund proven approaches for longer.** If something already has good evidence behind it, a short trial is a test of the funding, not the idea.
- **Judge on the right timeline.** Ask what has changed for people after several years, not just how many contacts happened this quarter.
- **Keep the people.** Give staff enough certainty to stay. In support work, the relationship is the service.
- **Write it down before it ends.** If a program must close, pay for it to record what worked so the next one starts ahead, not from zero.

## What you can take from this

If you work in or around services, or you vote, donate, or sit on a board:

1. When a program that works is about to end, ask what it will cost when the people it helps lose that support.
2. Back longer funding for things that already have evidence behind them.
3. Value the workers who stay. Continuity is not a luxury in this work.

Cutting a program that works doesn't save the money. It just moves the bill somewhere harder to see.
