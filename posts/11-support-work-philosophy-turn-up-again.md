# Turn Up Again Tomorrow

Why showing up, again and again, is the core of support work

A lot of support work looks like nothing much. A chat on a bench. A coffee. A walk to the chemist. Coming back next week at the same time, even when last week went badly.

This essay follows one thread of the street-level approach: that being reliable is not the warm-up to real support. It is the support.

## Trust is built in small, boring steps

Many people who end up sleeping rough or cycling through crisis services have been let down, a lot. Workers left. Appointments were cancelled. Promises were made and not kept. So when a new person turns up offering help, the sensible move is to wait and see.

You cannot talk someone out of that caution. You can only earn your way past it. That happens slowly:

- You say you will be there on Tuesday, and you are there on Tuesday.
- You say you will find out about that form, and you come back with the answer.
- You say "I don't know", when you don't know.
- You do not disappear when things get messy.

None of this is clever. All of it counts.

## Meet people where they are

"Meet people where they are" gets said so often it can lose its meaning. In plain terms it means two things.

First, literally go to where people are: the park, the street, the car they sleep in, the boarding house. Do not wait for them to find an office during business hours.

Second, start with what the person wants help with, not what you think their problem is. If someone wants a phone charger and a hot meal, start there. The bigger conversations come later, if they come at all, and they go better when the small things were handled well.

This is close to the idea behind harm reduction: keep people safer and alive now, without making help depend on them changing first. A good public overview is the [Harm Reduction International explainer](https://hri.global/what-is-harm-reduction/).

## Apply the inverse rule

The usual story is that a person who keeps "not engaging" is the problem. They miss appointments, they do not follow the plan, so they get closed off the books.

The inverse rule says: check the opposite. What if the service is the thing not engaging? Maybe the appointment was across town with no bus fare. Maybe the letter went to an address they no longer have. Maybe the last three workers left and they stopped bothering to learn new names.

When someone keeps dropping out, the first question is not "what is wrong with them?" It is "what are we asking that does not fit their life?"

## Do not close the door

Services often have rules that end support after a few missed contacts. Those rules make the numbers tidy. They also tend to push out the people who need help most, because those are the people whose lives make attendance hardest.

A better default:

- Leave the door open. Make it easy to come back without starting from scratch.
- Keep the same worker where you can. Starting over with a stranger is a real cost.
- Follow up after a no-show, kindly, instead of logging it and moving on.

## Look after the people doing it

Turning up again and again is tiring. Workers carry a lot, and burnt-out workers leave, which breaks the very trust this whole approach depends on. Good supervision, fair workloads, and time to rest are not perks. They keep the help steady.

## Try this

- Only promise what you can do. Then do it.
- Pick a regular time and place, and stick to it.
- Ask "what would help today?" before you ask anything else.
- When someone misses a meeting, follow up rather than close the file.
- If you manage a team, protect your workers' time so they can stay in the job.
