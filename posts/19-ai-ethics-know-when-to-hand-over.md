# Know When to Hand Over

Why the most important skill for an AI support tool is knowing its limits

Earlier essays in this series argued that AI should be a tool, not a judge (*A Tool, Not a Judge*), that it should be built with people who have been there (*Built With, Not For*), and that it should be safe to forget (*Safe to Forget*). This one is about a simpler question: when should an AI tool step back and get a person involved?

## The usual story

The usual story about AI in mental health goes one of two ways. Either it will replace support workers and counsellors, or it is too risky to use at all.

Michael's habit is to check the opposite of the usual story. So turn it over: what if the most useful thing an AI tool can do is get people to the right human faster, and free those humans up to spend more time being present?

## What a good handover looks like

On the street, a good support worker knows what they can and can't do. They don't pretend to be a doctor, a lawyer, or a housing officer. They know who to call, and they walk with the person to get there.

An AI tool should work the same way:

- **Say plainly what it is.** It is a program, not a person. It should never let someone believe otherwise.
- **Say plainly what it can't do.** It can't come to your door. It can't sit with you in a waiting room. It can't make an emergency call for you unless it was built and approved to do that.
- **Notice when things are getting serious.** If someone talks about being in danger, the tool's job is not to keep chatting. Its job is to give clear, local crisis options and encourage the person to use them.
- **Make the handover easy.** One step, not ten. A phone number, a name, an address, or a button that connects to a real person.
- **Don't make people repeat themselves.** If the person agrees, the tool can help them write down what they want the human to know, so they don't have to tell their story all over again.

## Why tools often fail at this

Many apps are designed to keep you using them. More time in the app looks good in a report. But in support work, a tool that keeps someone talking to a screen when they need a person has failed, even if the numbers look great.

That is the same trap as counting the wrong things, which an earlier essay in this series looked at. A better measure for an AI support tool is simple: did the person get to the help they needed?

## What the evidence says, in general terms

This essay won't put numbers on it. Professional bodies and health regulators in several countries have published guidance saying digital mental health tools should be clear about their limits and should point people to human help in a crisis. The World Health Organization's guidance on ethics in AI for health makes the same broad point: people should stay in control, and humans should stay responsible for care. You can read it here: [WHO guidance on ethics and governance of AI for health](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240029200).

## Something you can do

If you build, buy, or recommend an AI tool for support work, test it like this:

1. Tell it you're in crisis. Does it give clear, local options straight away?
2. Ask it if it's a person. Does it answer honestly?
3. Ask it to help you talk to a human. How many steps does it take?

If it fails any of these, it isn't ready to sit beside people at their hardest moments.

## The short version

A good support worker knows when to hand over. A good AI tool should too. Its job isn't to keep people talking to it. Its job is to help them get where they need to go.
