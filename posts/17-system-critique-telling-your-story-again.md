# Telling Your Story Again

Notes on why people in need get passed from service to service, and what a "no wrong door" approach looks like

Ask anyone who has needed a lot of help at once (housing, health, money, legal trouble) what wore them down the most. Many will not name the problem itself. They will name the process: telling their story again, and again, to a new person behind a new desk, who then sends them somewhere else.

This essay is about that pattern, why it happens, and what can be done about it.

## One person, many doors

Real problems come in bundles. Losing a home can lead to losing a job, which makes mental health worse, which makes it harder to keep appointments, which leads to losing a payment. But services are usually split up by topic. Each one deals with its own piece.

So a person with five linked problems may end up with five services, five intake forms, five waiting lists, and five workers who each only see one slice. Nobody holds the whole picture except the person who is least able to manage it right then.

## What it costs

Each retelling costs something:

- **Energy.** Repeating hard parts of your life to strangers is tiring, and for some people it brings back pain each time.
- **Time.** Every handover adds another wait.
- **Trust.** After being sent away a few times, many people stop asking. On paper that can look like they "did not engage". In reality, they gave up on a maze.

The system pays as well. Duplicated assessments are work done twice. And people who drop out between doors often come back later in a crisis, when help is harder and more expensive to give.

## Checking the opposite

Our usual habit applies: when everyone says "specialist services are best", check the opposite.

Specialists matter. Some problems need deep skill. But a system made only of specialists, with nobody whose job is to hold the whole person, works well for problems and badly for people. The opposite question is worth asking: who in this system is responsible for the person, not just the problem?

## What a "no wrong door" approach looks like

The idea is simple: wherever someone first asks for help, that should be a right door.

- **Warm handovers, not referrals on paper.** The first worker walks the person to the next service, or calls with them, and passes on the story with their permission, so they do not have to start from zero.
- **One steady contact.** One worker who stays with the person across services and keeps the thread.
- **Shared information, with consent.** The person decides what gets passed on. But once they agree, they should not have to fill in the same details again.
- **Services under one roof where possible.** Health, housing, and money help in the same place, on the same day.
- **Flexible rules at the edges.** If someone does not quite fit a service's criteria, the default is to help first and sort out the paperwork after.

None of this needs new technology. It needs services to see themselves as parts of one path rather than separate shops.

## Try this

If you work in a service, count how many times a person has to tell their story before they get real help. Then pick one handover you could make warm instead of cold.

If you have been through the maze yourself, your account of it is evidence. Services that want to improve should be asking for it.
