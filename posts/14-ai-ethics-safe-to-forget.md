# Safe to Forget

Privacy, memory, and trust in AI tools for mental health support

When someone tells a support worker something hard, they are trusting that worker with it. A good worker knows what to write down, what to keep to themselves, and what to let go of. Trust is built as much on what is *not* passed on as on what is.

AI tools for mental health support are now hearing the same kinds of things. This essay is about what they should do with them.

## The inverse rule, applied to data

This project keeps coming back to one habit: when everyone agrees on a story, check the opposite.

The common story in tech is that more data makes a better product. Remember everything, learn from everything, and the tool gets smarter.

The opposite is worth checking. In support work, a tool that remembers everything can make people *less* safe. Someone who is scared their words will be stored, shared, or used against them will not say the thing that matters. Then the tool, however clever, is working with half the story.

## What "safe" means to the person talking

People in hard times often have good reasons to be careful. They may worry about:

- a past conversation turning up somewhere it should not, such as with a landlord, an employer, or a government office
- being labelled by a record they never got to see
- someone else picking up their phone and reading what they wrote
- their worst night being used to train a product or sell them something

None of these worries are strange. A street-level approach takes them seriously instead of asking people to "just trust the system".

## Plain rules for a tool people can trust

**Say what you keep, in one sentence.** Not a long policy page. Something like: "I keep this chat for 30 days so you can come back to it. You can delete it any time." If a tool cannot explain its own memory simply, that is a warning sign.

**Keep as little as you need.** If the tool only needs to remember someone's preferred name and the services near them, it should not also be storing every word of every conversation.

**Let people see it and wipe it.** A clear "show me what you remember" and "forget this" button does more for trust than any promise.

**Never use someone's crisis to sell.** No ads, upsells, or "engagement" nudges built on what a person shared when they were struggling.

**Ask before passing anything on.** If the tool helps connect someone to a worker or service, it should say exactly what will be shared and wait for a yes, except in the narrow cases where someone's life is in immediate danger, and then it should be honest about that too.

**Have a quick-exit option.** On a shared phone or computer, people need a way to close the tool fast and leave no obvious trace.

## Why this saves money as well as harm

It can sound like privacy is a cost: more features, more work. But a tool people do not trust gets used once and dropped. People go back to waiting until things are bad enough for the emergency department, which is the most expensive and least gentle door in the system. A tool that is safe to talk to has a chance of helping earlier, and earlier help is almost always cheaper help.

## Something you can do this week

If you build or choose AI tools for a service: write down, in one plain sentence, what the tool remembers and for how long. If you cannot, find out before anyone in crisis uses it.

If you use these tools yourself: look for the settings page and check whether you can see and delete your history. If you cannot, think of it as a public noticeboard, not a private diary, and keep the most personal things for a person you trust.
