# Start With Tonight, Not the File

A street-level philosophy of support work
People in distress are often asked to become a case before they are allowed to become a neighbour. The file comes first. The diagnosis. The referral. The proof that they are "ready." Meanwhile the night is still the night, and the person in front of you still needs a place to sleep, something to eat, and a reason to trust you will not disappear.

This is a public note on a way of working that starts the other way around. It is not a clinical manual. It is a set of habits that anyone doing support work, paid or unpaid, can try.
## Meet the person, not the category

Are you safe tonight? Do you have a key? Have you eaten? Is anyone after you? Do you want company, or do you want to be left alone with a meal and a phone number? You can learn more in ten honest minutes on a street or a jetty than in a form written for a funding body. The form may still need filling. It should not be the first conversation, and it should not be the price of help.

Lived experience belongs here as evidence, not as decoration. People who have been through services can see the traps that look like policy from an office. If you only want their story for a brochure, you are not doing support work. You are mining it.
## The inverse rule

When a tidy official story appears — "they refuse help," "they are not ready," "treatment first, then housing" — turn it over and test the opposite. Maybe the help is the wrong shape. Maybe "not ready" means the door has a lock they cannot open. Maybe they did not refuse help. They refused a particular kind of help, from a system that had already let them down.

The inverse rule is not a claim that institutions are always wrong. It is a habit of checking. If you accept the official version without testing it against the person in front of you, you will keep repeating the official failure.
## A home is not a prize for getting well

A lot of public policy still treats housing as a reward for recovery: get clean, take the medication, attend the groups, then maybe you can have a key. That sequence sounds orderly. It is also backwards for many people.

Housing First is a well-studied alternative: give someone a stable place to live without making sobriety or treatment a condition of the keys, and then offer support. Canada's At Home/Chez Soi project, the world's largest trial of this approach, followed more than two thousand people in five cities. People offered Housing First spent much more of their time in stable housing than people left in usual services — in a published review of that trial, about seventy-three percent of the time, compared with about thirty-two percent.

Read the Mental Health Commission of Canada's account at [At Home / Chez Soi](https://mentalhealthcommission.ca/what-we-do/at-home/), a [research review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4679127/), and the original New York model at the [Pathways Housing First Institute](https://www.pathwayshousingfirst.org/).

A key is not a gold star. It is the ground that everything else stands on. You cannot reasonably ask someone to "engage" while they are still negotiating where they will sleep.
## Help that is clear, fair, and close in time

People change when change is possible, not when they are lectured. If you want a behaviour to happen more often, notice it quickly and reward it in a way the person actually values.

That is the core of contingency management, sometimes called motivational incentives. It is an honest deal: a reachable goal, a fair check, and a real reward that arrives soon after. The U.S. National Institute on Drug Abuse describes it as a behavioural treatment that uses rewards for staying in counselling, taking medicine as agreed, or remaining drug-free, and it is among the best-supported approaches we have for cocaine and methamphetamine use. See NIDA's [treatment and recovery guide](https://nida.nih.gov/publications/drugs-brains-behavior-science-addiction/treatment-recovery) and its [cocaine page](https://nida.nih.gov/research-topics/cocaine).

In ordinary support work the same idea holds. Be specific. Be quick. Do not move the goalposts.
## Count the whole bill

Systems often congratulate themselves for saving money in the wrong column. A cheap outreach shift is cut. A boarding house closes. A worker's hours shrink. The clinic budget looks tighter and more "efficient." The costs do not vanish. They move — into emergency departments, police vans, evictions, and cells.

That is the false economy of many mental health systems. If you only measure the program in front of you, you will keep making decisions that look prudent and fail in the street. When someone wants to cut a simple, human thing because it is hard to count, ask where the cost will land next.
## Rooms are not the only place people get well

Some people settle in a clinic chair. Many do not. Time on the water, time outdoors, and skilled work with the hands can do what a waiting room cannot. Call it ecotherapy or blue water therapy if a name helps a funder. The practice is older than the name: a walk, a deck, a workshop, light moving across glass.

This is why a 1945 timber Navy vessel, slowly turned into a place to live, and the making of dichroic glass — glass that shifts colour with the light — belong in a public account of this work. They are not hobbies parked beside the "real" job. A life is larger than a service plan.

The same is true of humour. A life often looks more like a comedy skit than a case history. Telling it that way, as in the autobiography idea "My Life as a Comedy Skit," is a way of staying honest without turning a person into a tragedy for other people to manage. Laugh with people. Never at them. Do not tidy the plot.
## If you build tools, including AI, keep the person in charge

A good tool helps a person think, remember, plan, or reach a human being. A bad one diagnoses in secret, shames, harvests private distress, or pretends to be a friend or a clinician it is not. Design for support, not for judgement. Make the limits obvious. Keep a human in the loop. Do not make a person's housing, money, or liberty depend on a model they cannot question.
## What to do tomorrow morning

Ask what would make tonight safer before you ask for a diagnosis. Offer a key, a meal, a lift, a quiet hour, or a real choice before you offer a program. If someone says no, treat that as information about the offer, not as proof they do not want help. If a meeting is about a person who is not in the room, change that if you can. Follow public evidence where it is strong. Do not use anyone's crisis, family, or legal situation as content.

None of this requires a new committee. It requires the nerve to start with the person in front of you, and the humility to check the opposite of the story you were handed.
