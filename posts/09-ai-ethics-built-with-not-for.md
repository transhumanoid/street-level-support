# Built With, Not For

Why people with lived experience should help design AI for mental health support

When a new mental health app or chatbot is built, the people in the room are usually developers, product managers, and sometimes clinicians. The people it is meant to help are often only there as "users" in a test at the end.

That is backwards. If a tool is going to sit with someone on their worst night, the people who have had those nights should help shape it from the start. This essay is about what that looks like in practice.

## The inverse rule, applied to AI

This project keeps coming back to one habit: when everyone agrees on a story, check the opposite.

The common story about AI in mental health is that the hard part is the technology. Make the model smarter, and the help gets better.

The opposite is worth checking. Maybe the hard part is not the model. Maybe it is knowing what actually helps when someone is struggling: what words land, what feels like being judged, what makes a person close the app and never come back. That knowledge does not live in code. It lives in people who have been through it, and in the workers who sat beside them.

## What lived experience catches that others miss

People who have used services tend to notice things a design team will not:

- **The tone of being managed.** A cheerful script can feel like a door being politely shut. People who have heard "have you tried breathing exercises?" at the wrong moment know exactly how that lands.
- **The fear of records.** Many people worry about what is written down about them and who will see it. A tool that quietly stores everything can feel less like support and more like a file being built.
- **The real barriers.** No phone credit. A shared phone. No quiet place to talk. Reading is hard today. A tool built only for a calm person with a new phone and good Wi-Fi misses the people who need it most.
- **What "help" means.** Sometimes help is a crisis line. Often it is a reminder to eat, a way to find a free meal tonight, or someone to draft a message to a landlord.

## What "built with" actually means

Asking a few people for feedback after the tool is finished is not co-design. Built with means:

**Paid seats at the table.** People with lived experience are part of the team and are paid for their time, the same as any other expert.

**Early, not late.** They help decide what the tool is for, not just what colour the buttons are.

**Power to say no.** If a feature feels like surveillance, or like it pushes people to keep chatting for the sake of numbers, they can stop it.

**Ongoing, not one-off.** People's needs change, and so do the tools. The group keeps meeting after launch, and keeps reading real (consented, de-identified) feedback.

## Privacy is part of care

In support work, trust is everything. If a person thinks what they say will be used against them, they stop saying it.

So a support tool should keep as little as it needs, say plainly what it keeps and why, and let people delete their history easily. It should never sell or share what people tell it for advertising. If a tool can't explain its privacy rules in two plain sentences, that is a warning sign.

## Measure what matters

It is easy to measure how long people spend in an app. It is much harder, and much more useful, to ask whether they got what they came for. Did they find a bed? Did they reach a person? Did they feel less alone, and did they feel free to leave?

A tool designed with lived experience will usually choose the harder measure. That choice tells you a lot about who it was built for.

## Something you can do this week

If you build or buy digital mental health tools, ask one question at your next meeting: "Who on this project has used services like the ones we are replacing or supporting, and are they paid and listened to?"

If you have lived experience yourself, your view is expertise. Many services and research groups run lived-experience advisory groups. Joining one is a real way to shape what gets built next.
