# A Tool, Not a Judge

What a street-level approach asks of AI for mental health support

More and more people now talk to a chatbot when things get hard. Some do it at 3am because nobody else is awake. Some do it because a waiting list is months long.

So the useful question is not "should AI be part of mental health support?" It is "if it is going to be there anyway, what should it be like?" This is a note on that question, from the same street-level point of view as the rest of this project: start with what the person in front of you actually needs, and check the tidy story before you believe it.

## Start with the person, not the product

Support work starts with plain questions. Are you safe tonight? Have you eaten? Do you want company? A good AI tool should start in the same place. It should help someone think, remember, plan, or reach a human being. It should not try to sort them into a box.

That means the tool is a support, not a judge. It should not diagnose in secret, keep a hidden score on someone, or decide what they "really" mean. The person stays in charge of their own story.

## Be honest about limits

The most important thing an AI support tool can do is tell the truth about what it is.

It is not a therapist. It is not a friend in the full sense of the word. It does not know the person's life, and it can be confidently wrong. It can miss danger, and it can also see danger where there is none. A tool that hides these limits behind a warm voice is not being kind. It is being misleading.

Say clearly what the tool can help with. Say what it cannot do. Say when it is unsure. If it is drafting, guessing, or summarising, say that too.

## Hand off to humans, and make it easy

A street-level approach never treats one service as the whole answer. The same goes for software.

A good tool knows when to step back. If someone talks about being in danger, the tool should help them reach a person: a crisis line, a local service, someone they trust. And it should do that in a way that works. Give the real number for where they are. Do not just say "please seek help" and carry on as before.

Handing off should not feel like being dumped. It should feel like being walked to the door of the right place. And the tool should not quietly try to keep people talking because engagement looks good on a dashboard. Sometimes the best thing a chatbot can say is "this needs a human, and here is how to reach one now."

## Privacy is part of care

When someone tells a tool about their worst night, that is not "user data." It is a confidence.

Collect as little as possible. Keep it only as long as it is needed. Tell people plainly where it goes and who can see it. Never use private distress to train systems, target advertising, or build profiles without clear, informed consent that the person can actually understand and can take back.

The World Health Organization makes a similar point in its [guidance on the ethics and governance of AI for health](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240029200). Its first principle is protecting human autonomy: humans stay in control of health decisions, privacy and confidentiality are protected, and people give valid informed consent. You can read the [six principles in WHO's own summary](https://www.who.int/news/item/28-06-2021-who-issues-first-global-report-on-ai-in-health-and-six-guiding-principles-for-its-design-and-use).

## Do not replace people

There is a tempting story that AI can fill the gaps in mental health services. Waiting lists are long, workers are scarce, and software never gets tired.

Apply the inverse rule and turn that story around. Maybe the gaps are not a shortage of chat. Maybe they are a shortage of housing, of time, of workers who are paid enough to stay, of places where someone can just sit and be safe. A chatbot cannot hand over a key, drive someone to an appointment, or notice that a person has stopped turning up at the bench where they usually sit. People do those things.

So the right job for AI is to support human help, not stand in for it. It can help a worker with paperwork so they have more time with people. It can be a bridge at 3am until morning comes. It should not become the reason a service decides it no longer needs the humans.

## The false economy of cheap automation

This is where the money story comes in, and it is the same pattern as the rest of this project.

Replacing human support with a cheap app can look like a saving. The budget line for staff goes down. But if the app misses someone in danger, gives bad advice, or simply leaves people feeling more alone, the cost does not disappear. It turns up later, somewhere else: in an emergency department, a police call, a lost tenancy, a hospital bed. The saving is made where someone is counting, and the bill is paid where nobody is.

The honest test for any AI tool is not "how much does it cost?" It is "what happens to the whole bill, and to the people, when we use it?" WHO's guidance also asks that AI tools be assessed openly during real use, not only before launch. That is a good rule for anyone buying or building these systems.

## What to build, in plain terms

1. **Say what it is.** A tool, with limits, stated up front and repeated when it matters.
2. **Keep the person in charge.** No hidden scores, no secret diagnoses, no pressure.
3. **Make the human door easy.** Real local contacts, offered early, without a lecture.
4. **Protect confidences.** Collect little, keep it short, never train on distress without real consent.
5. **Support workers, do not replace them.** Measure success by human time freed up, not human jobs cut.
6. **Count the whole bill.** Check what happens after the chat ends, not just what the chat cost.
7. **Ask people with lived experience.** They will spot the traps in a design long before a review board does.

## The short version

AI can be useful in mental health support if it is honest, modest, and built to bring people closer to other people. It goes wrong when it pretends to be more than it is, keeps people talking instead of helping them, treats their pain as data, or becomes an excuse to cut the human help that actually changes lives. Before you accept that automation is the efficient choice, turn the story over and add up the whole bill.
