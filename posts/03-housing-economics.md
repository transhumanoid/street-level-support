# Keys Before Conditions

A plain look at the economics of housing people first

For a long time, the usual way to help someone who was homeless looked like a staircase. First you got off the street and into a shelter. Then you got sober, took your medication, and went to your groups. If you kept that up, you might move into transitional housing. And if you kept it up there, one day you might get a key to a place of your own.

That staircase sounds sensible. It also loses a lot of people on every step. This is a note on why, what the evidence says about doing it the other way around, and why the "cheaper" option is often the expensive one.

## The staircase and the false economy

The staircase is built on an idea that sounds like good budgeting: do not spend scarce housing on people who might "waste" it. Make them prove they are ready first.

Turn it over and look at what that idea costs. A person waiting at the bottom of the staircase does not stop needing things. They still get sick, still get hurt, still get moved on, still get arrested for things that are mostly about having nowhere to be. Each of those moments has a price: an ambulance, an emergency department visit, a night in a cell, a court date, a hospital bed, a shelter bed, a worker's time chasing someone who has moved again.

None of those costs show up in the housing budget. So the housing program can say it saved money by being careful. The rest of the system quietly pays the bill.

That is the false economy. It is the same pattern as cutting a cheap community service and then paying for the crisis it used to prevent. The money is saved where people are counting, and spent where they are not.

## The inverse rule: what if the key comes first?

Michael's working habit is what he calls the inverse rule: when the mainstream story sounds tidy, test the opposite before you accept it.

The tidy story here is "housing is a reward for recovery." The opposite is "housing is the ground recovery stands on." It is hard to keep appointments with no address, hard to store medication with no cupboard, hard to sleep well enough to make good decisions when you might be moved on at 3am. Asking someone to get well first and get a home later is asking them to do the hardest part with the fewest tools.

Housing First is the name for testing that opposite in practice. The basic deal is simple:

- Offer a person a home of their own, in ordinary housing, without making sobriety or treatment a condition of the keys.
- Offer support alongside it: a worker who visits, help with health, money, and daily life, and treatment if and when the person wants it.
- Treat the person as a tenant with normal rights, not as a patient on probation.

The model is usually traced to Pathways to Housing in New York in the early 1990s. The [Pathways Housing First Institute](https://www.pathwayshousingfirst.org/) still describes and teaches it.

## What the evidence says

Housing First is not just a nice idea. It has been tested more carefully than most social programs ever are.

The best-known test is Canada's At Home/Chez Soi project. It was a large randomised trial across five cities, run by the Mental Health Commission of Canada. People who were homeless and living with mental illness were randomly offered either Housing First or the usual services in their city, and then followed over time. You can read the [Commission's overview](https://mentalhealthcommission.ca/what-we-do/at-home/) and the [national final report](https://mentalhealthcommission.ca/resource/national-at-home-chez-soi-final-report/).

The headline finding was clear. People offered Housing First got housed faster and stayed housed much more of the time. A [published research review of the trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4679127/) reports that people in the Housing First group spent about 73% of their time in stable housing over the two-year study, compared with about 32% for people in usual services.

That matters because the staircase assumes people who have not "earned" housing will lose it. In the trial, many of them did not. Given a key and support, a lot of people kept their homes.

The project also looked at costs. The general picture from that work, and from Housing First programs elsewhere, is that a large part of the cost of housing and supporting people is offset by less use of other services, such as shelters, hospitals, and the justice system. How much is offset depends on the place and the people. It tends to be strongest for people who were using the most expensive emergency services before. If you want to make a cost argument in your own area, look up the local numbers. Do not borrow someone else's figure and pretend it is yours.

## Where contingency management fits

Housing First answers the question "where does someone live while they change?" It does not, on its own, answer "how do you help someone change their drug use if they want to?"

One well-supported answer to the second question is contingency management. Again, the idea is plain: agree on a goal someone can actually reach, check it fairly, and give a real reward soon after they meet it. Vouchers, small payments, or privileges, delivered reliably. It has particularly strong evidence for stimulants such as cocaine and methamphetamine, where there are few medication options. The U.S. National Institute on Drug Abuse describes it in its [guide to treatment and recovery](https://nida.nih.gov/publications/drugs-brains-behavior-science-addiction/treatment-recovery).

The two ideas fit together because they share a logic. Both reward people for what they do, in the present, instead of punishing them for who they have been. And both reject the staircase. Contingency management gives the reward for a small reachable step. It does not withhold everything until the whole climb is done.

## Why the staircase survives anyway

If the evidence is this strong, why do so many systems still run on conditions?

- **Budgets live in silos.** The housing agency pays for housing. The health system pays for hospitals. The justice system pays for cells. Nobody owns the whole bill, so nobody gets credit for shrinking it.
- **Conditions feel fair.** "Why should they get a home if they will not stop using?" is a moral question dressed up as an economic one. It feels like protecting the public purse. Often it does the opposite.
- **Failure is blamed on the person.** When someone falls off the staircase, the record says they "did not comply." It rarely says the staircase was the wrong shape.
- **Short-term thinking.** Housing costs money now. The savings show up later and somewhere else.

## What to do with this

1. **Ask what it costs to leave someone without a home.** Not as a figure of speech. Ask other services what they spend.
2. **Separate the key from the conditions.** Offer support generously. Do not make it the price of a door that locks.
3. **Reward steps, not perfection.** If someone is working on drug use, a fair, reachable goal and a reliable reward beats a lecture.
4. **Keep checking the opposite.** If a policy story says "they are not ready," ask what being ready would actually require, and whether the system is the thing standing in the way.
5. **Use real evidence, honestly.** Quote what you have read. Admit where the numbers are local or uncertain.

## The short version

A home is not a prize for getting well. For many people, it is the thing that makes getting well possible. The careful-sounding option of making people earn housing often costs more, because the bill turns up in hospitals, shelters, and cells instead of in the housing budget. Before you accept that conditions are prudent, turn the story over and add up the whole bill.
