# Ask What Would Help

Notes on the simplest question in support work, and why it is so often skipped

Most people who reach out for help have already been told what they need. By family, by services, by forms with boxes to tick. Very few have been asked a plain question and then actually listened to:

**"What would help you most right now?"**

This essay is about that question: why it matters, why systems skip it, and how to ask it well.

## Why the question matters

People usually know their own lives better than anyone else does. They might not have the words for it, and they might not have the full picture. But they often know what is pressing hardest today.

Sometimes the answer surprises the worker. Someone in crisis might not want to talk about their mental health at all. They might want a phone charger, a hot shower, help with a fine, or a lift to see their dog. Sorting out that one thing builds more trust than an hour of assessment questions.

Once the most pressing thing is handled, people often have room to talk about the bigger things. Not before.

## Why systems skip it

Services are usually set up around what they offer, not around what people ask for. A clinic offers appointments. A housing service offers a list. A program offers its program. So the first conversation becomes a sorting exercise: which box does this person fit in?

That is understandable. Workers are busy, and funding is tied to specific outcomes. But it means people learn to say whatever gets them through the door, rather than what is really going on. The service then wonders why its plans do not stick.

## Checking the opposite

Our usual habit applies: when everyone assumes the professional knows best, check the opposite.

Professionals know a lot. They know the options, the risks, and the rules. But they do not live the person's life. A plan built only on what the professional thinks is needed often fails quietly, because it does not fit the person's day.

The honest middle is this: the person says what matters to them, and the worker brings what they know about how to get there. Both kinds of knowledge count.

There are real limits, too. If someone is in immediate danger, safety comes first. And some people will ask for things a worker cannot give. Being clear about that, kindly and early, is part of respect.

## How to ask it well

- **Ask early.** Before the forms, not after.
- **Ask plainly.** "What would make today a bit easier?" works better than "What are your goals?"
- **Take the answer seriously.** Even if it seems small. Small is often where things start.
- **Do something about it, soon.** Asking and then doing nothing teaches people that asking is pointless.
- **Ask again later.** What matters changes. The question is not a one-off.

## Try this

Next time you sit with someone, at work or just as a friend, hold back your first idea about what they need. Ask what would help. Then listen to the whole answer before you say anything.

It is a small habit. It changes a lot.
