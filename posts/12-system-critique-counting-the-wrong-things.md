# Counting the Wrong Things

How what a system measures quietly decides who it helps

Every service gets judged by numbers. How many people were seen. How fast the waiting list moved. How many cases were closed this quarter. Those numbers decide funding, contracts, and whose job is safe.

This essay follows one thread of the street-level approach: when a system counts the wrong things, it ends up working for the numbers instead of the people.

## What gets counted gets done

Numbers are not neutral. Once a team is judged on a measure, people start shaping their work around it, often without meaning to. There is an old idea for this, usually called Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. A plain introduction is on [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goodhart%27s_law).

In mental health and homelessness services, that can look like:

- Short appointments, because "people seen" goes up when each visit is quicker.
- Closing files after a few missed contacts, because open cases that "do not engage" look bad.
- Picking the people most likely to succeed, because good outcome rates win the next contract.
- Moving someone on to another service, because a referral counts as a result.

Each of these makes the report look better. None of them makes life better for the person who is struggling most.

## Apply the inverse rule

The usual story is that more measurement means more accountability. The inverse rule says: check the opposite.

What if some measures make services less accountable to the people they serve? A team can hit every target while the person sleeping in the car park gets nothing, because that person was never going to make the numbers look good. The target is met. The need is not.

## Things that matter but are hard to count

Some of the most important parts of support barely show up in a spreadsheet:

- A person trusting a worker enough to come back.
- Someone keeping their home for another year.
- A crisis that did not happen because help came early.
- A person feeling treated like a human being.

Prevention is the hardest of all to count, because success is something that did not happen. That is one reason systems keep underfunding it and then paying more later for emergencies, hospital stays, and police time.

## Better questions to ask

You do not fix this by throwing out numbers. You fix it by counting things that line up with people's lives:

- Did the person get what they came for, in their own words?
- Are people still housed, still in contact, still safe, months later?
- Who did we turn away or lose, and why?
- What do the people using the service say would make it better?

And ask people with lived experience to help choose the measures in the first place. They know which numbers reflect real help and which ones just look tidy.

## Try this

- If you run a service, list your main targets and ask, for each one: could we hit this while helping people less? If yes, add a measure that catches that.
- Track who leaves or gets closed off, not just who succeeds.
- Ask people directly whether the help helped, and report what they say.
- If you fund services, reward keeping hard cases, not avoiding them.
