# Fixable by Hand

Notes on converting an old timber boat to electric power, and why the parts you can repair yourself matter more than the parts that look impressive

There is an old timber boat in this project, built in 1945 as a Navy vessel, about 35 feet long. It has outlived most of the people who first worked on it. The plan is to move it from a diesel engine to electric power. This essay is about one choice inside that plan, and why it says something about how support work should be built too.

## The choice: simple over shiny

When people talk about electric boats, they often picture the newest, most efficient gear: sealed units, clever electronics, an app on your phone. That gear can be very good. It can also be very hard to fix when something goes wrong a long way from a workshop.

So the plan here goes the other way. The idea is to use a drive taken from the world of forklifts, with brushed electric motors.

- **Forklift drives are built to work hard.** They are made to start, stop, and push heavy loads all day in warehouses. That is not so different from what a boat motor does.
- **They are common.** Forklifts are everywhere, so parts, second-hand units, and people who understand them are not hard to find.
- **Brushed motors can be serviced.** The brushes are parts that wear out by design. When they wear down, you replace them. It is a job you can learn to do yourself with basic tools.

A brushed motor is older, plainer technology. It is not the most efficient option on paper. But a motor you can fix at the dock is worth more than a better motor you have to ship away and wait for.

## Checking the opposite

Our usual habit applies: when everyone says "newest is best", check the opposite.

Newer is often better. But "better" depends on what you need. If you need something that keeps going, that you understand, and that you can repair without a specialist, then simple and well-known can beat clever and sealed. The real question is not "what is most advanced?" It is "what happens on the day it breaks?"

There are honest downsides too:

- **Brushes wear.** That means regular checks and spare brushes on board.
- **Efficiency matters on a boat.** Every bit of wasted power is battery you do not have. Planning has to account for that.
- **Electrics and salt water are a serious mix.** Wiring, fuses, and ventilation have to be done properly, to the rules, by people who know what they are doing. Nobody should learn electrical safety by trial and error on the water.

## Why this matters beyond the boat

The same rule shows up in support work.

A lot of mental health and social support is built like a sealed unit. It looks impressive. It has forms, systems, and software. But when it breaks for one person (they miss an appointment, they lose their phone, they move), nobody nearby can fix it. They get sent back to the start of a waiting list.

Good street-level support is more like a brushed motor:

- **Plain parts.** A person who turns up, a phone number that works, a place to sit.
- **Wear is expected.** People slip, miss things, and come back. The service is built for that instead of punishing it.
- **Fixable on the spot.** The worker in front of you can actually solve the problem, without waiting for a head office far away.

## What you can take from this

If you are building anything people will depend on (a boat, a service, a piece of software), ask three questions:

1. When it breaks, who can fix it, and how far away are they?
2. Which parts are meant to wear out, and are the spares close by?
3. Would someone with basic tools and a bit of training be able to keep it running?

If the honest answers are "a specialist", "nobody planned for that", and "no", it might be worth choosing something plainer.

The old boat has lasted more than eighty years because people could keep fixing it. That is the standard worth aiming for.
