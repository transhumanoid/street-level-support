# A Bed Is Not a Home

Notes on the difference between a place to sleep tonight and a place to live, and why the cheap-looking option is often the expensive one

When someone has nowhere to go, the usual answer is a bed for the night. A shelter bunk, a crisis bed, a few nights in a motel paid by the week. It is fast, and on a cold night it can be the difference between safe and not safe. Nobody is arguing against that.

The problem starts when the bed for tonight becomes the plan for the next year.

## How the churn works

Emergency accommodation is built to be short. That is its job. But when there is nowhere to move on to, people end up cycling through it:

- **A few nights here, a few nights there.** Each stay ends, and the search starts again.
- **Nothing can be planned.** It is hard to keep a job, a medication routine, or a school place when you don't know where you will sleep next week.
- **Belongings and documents get lost.** Every move is a chance to lose ID, phones, and paperwork, which then takes weeks to replace.
- **Support has to keep finding people.** Workers spend their time locating someone instead of helping them.

Each of those nights is paid for. Add them up over months, and the total can be large, with very little to show at the end.

## Checking the opposite

Our usual habit applies: when everyone says "emergency beds are the affordable option", check the opposite.

A motel room by the night is often one of the most expensive ways to house someone. A shelter bed looks cheap per night, but it doesn't end anything. The person is still homeless when they walk out in the morning, and the crisis costs that come with homelessness (ambulances, emergency departments, police time) keep coming.

This is the core idea behind Housing First: move people into ordinary, permanent housing with their own lease as early as possible, and bring support to them there. Public evaluations of Housing First programs in several countries have generally found that most people stay housed, and that some of the cost is offset by less use of crisis services. The exact figures depend on the place and the program, so it is worth reading the local evidence. But the direction is consistent: a home ends the cycle in a way a bed does not.

## What a home gives that a bed doesn't

- **An address.** You can get mail, apply for jobs, and be found by services.
- **A door that locks.** Sleep, safety, and somewhere to keep medication and papers.
- **Time.** Problems that took a back seat to "where do I sleep tonight" can finally get attention.
- **Ownership.** Your own lease is a different feeling from a bed you can lose for breaking a house rule.

## What you can take from this

If you work in services, sit on a board, or just care about this:

1. Keep emergency beds for emergencies, and ask what the move-on plan is from night one.
2. When you see money going to long motel stays, ask what the same money could do as rent and support in a real home.
3. Back programs that measure how many people stay housed, not just how many beds were filled.

A bed gets someone through the night. A home gets them through the year.
