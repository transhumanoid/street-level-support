# The Wrong Pocket

Why good housing programs get cut even when they save money

Earlier essays in this series made the case for Housing First (*Keys Before Conditions*), for keeping people housed (*Keeping a Home Is Cheaper Than Finding One*), and for treating the wait itself as a cost (*Waiting Is Not Free*). This one asks a harder question: if housing people works, and often costs less overall, why is it so hard to keep funded?

## The usual story

The usual story is that there simply isn't enough money. Budgets are tight, and housing is expensive.

Michael's habit is to check the opposite of the usual story. So turn it over: what if the money is there, but it is sitting in the wrong pocket?

## How the pockets work

A government is not one wallet. It is many separate budgets, each with its own manager, its own targets, and its own year-end report. Housing has one. Hospitals have another. Police, courts, and prisons have theirs. Mental health, drug and alcohol services, and child protection each have their own too.

Now picture what happens when someone who has been sleeping rough gets a home and steady support:

- The housing budget pays for the home and the support worker.
- Fewer emergency department visits show up in the hospital budget.
- Fewer arrests and nights in a cell show up in the police and justice budgets.
- Fewer crisis callouts show up in the ambulance and mental health budgets.

The cost lands in one pocket. The savings land in several others. The manager who has to find the money sees only the cost.

Economists sometimes call this the "wrong pocket problem". It is not a secret. It is just easy to ignore, because no single report shows the whole picture.

## What the evidence says, in general terms

This essay will not put numbers on it, because the real figures differ from place to place and made-up figures help nobody. But studies of Housing First in several countries, including the large Canadian At Home/Chez Soi trial, have found that housing people with support reduces their use of costly emergency services, and that a good share of the program's cost is offset elsewhere. Canada's Mental Health Commission has published the findings: [At Home/Chez Soi final report](https://mentalhealthcommission.ca/resource/national-final-report-cross-site-at-home-chez-soi-project/).

The offset is real. It just doesn't come back to the budget that paid.

## Why this matters on the street

For the person, none of these pockets exist. There is just their life: a bed or no bed, a doctor who knows them or a new one each time, a worker who stays or a worker who leaves when a program ends.

When funding is judged one pocket at a time, the programs that help most across the whole system look the most expensive. They get trimmed. Then the costs quietly move back to emergency rooms, police cells, and crisis teams, where they are often higher and the help is worse.

That is the false economy in a sentence: saving money in one place by spending more of it somewhere else.

## What could be done instead

None of these ideas are new or radical. They just need someone to act on them.

1. **Count across pockets.** When deciding whether to fund a housing program, look at what it changes in health, justice, and crisis budgets too, not only what it costs housing.
2. **Share the savings.** Let the budgets that save money help pay for the program that saved it. Some places have tried pooled funds or joint commissioning between housing and health.
3. **Fund for longer.** Short pilot funding ends just as the savings start to show. Multi-year funding lets the full picture appear.
4. **Ask people who use the services.** People who have moved between hospitals, cells, and shelters can often describe the whole chain of costs better than any one department.

## What you can do

If you work in a service, notice when a person's story crosses several systems, and say so in your reports. If you vote, ask candidates how they count the costs of homelessness, and whether they look past one department's budget. If you fund or run programs, ask who else benefits when your program works, and invite them to the table.

A home is not only a housing expense. It is one of the most practical health, safety, and justice investments there is. It just needs to be counted that way.
